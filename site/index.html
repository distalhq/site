The problem

Three ways one chip fails you.

A secure element is trusted on three things at once. Its silicon resists physical attack. Its vendor has no backdoor. Its supply chain delivered the genuine part. If any one of those fails, every device that relies on that chip alone fails with it.

Physical attack

In 2026, Ledger’s Donjon team used laser fault injection to run their own firmware on a TROPIC01 secure element. Certification measures resistance to the attacks a lab knows today.

Opaque vendors

Closed secure elements are evaluated under NDA. Trezor reports finding a widely used chip below its standards in 2019 and being barred from saying why.

Sovereignty and supply

Buyers increasingly ask whether a chip from one country can anchor critical infrastructure, and export controls can cut supply at short notice.

Layering contained the TROPIC01 break: unlocking still depended on a second secure element, the microcontroller and the PIN. Distal applies the same idea to signing itself. Sources: Ledger Donjon, Trezor.

How one signature happens

Chips sign. The host only combines.

  1. Something to signA payment, a firmware image, a certificate. Your device decides what gets signed.
  2. Secure element AVendor one. Holds share 1. Computes a partial signature.
    Secure element BVendor two. Holds share 2. Computes a partial signature.
  3. Your firmware combinesIt sees partial signatures only. There is no key in its memory to steal.
  4. One signatureA standard Schnorr signature. Verifiers cannot tell it came from two chips.

  • No whole key, ever

    Shares are created on the chips themselves. At setup, at signing and at recovery the full key never sits in one place, so there is nothing for malware or a probe on your microcontroller to read.

  • Chips that check each other

    PIN-retry limits stay in agreement across the chips, and every signature uses randomness committed by a peer, so a backdoored chip cannot leak its share slowly. This part is new cryptography: the specification is published and externally reviewed before it ships.

  • Recovery, 2 of 4

    Two shares live in the device on chips from two vendors. Two sit on offline cards kept apart. Any two sign. A chip dies: the rest make a new share. Device lost: the cards sign. Device stolen: the cards move everything to a new key.

  • Ordinary signatures out

    FROST threshold Schnorr (RFC 9591) produces standard signatures. Nothing changes for whoever verifies them. The first curve is secp256k1, the one Bitcoin Taproot uses.

A compromised host can still ask the chips to sign something you did not intend. Distal protects the key, not the decision to use it. Pair it with a trusted display or on-chip policy.

Why you can rely on it

Our code is the new single point of trust.

A vendor’s certificate covers its chip and operating system, not our applet. If one applet runs on both chips, one bug could touch both. Vendor diversity protects against silicon and vendor failure, not against our own mistakes, so we treat the applet as the thing to scrutinize.

Open source

The applet for JavaCard chips such as NXP JCOP and Infineon SECORA, the host library and the specification are public. Anyone can read what runs on the chips.

Reproducible builds

You can rebuild the applet from source and check that it matches what gets installed.

Side channels and audit

The applet gets its own side-channel evaluation and an external audit before it goes into a customer device.

Why “distal”

Farther from the center.

Signing belongs inside the device, at the edge of the system, not in a server or in a single trusted chip. Distal is the companion secure element that signs with another chip while each keeps its own share.

  1. First proof

    Taproot on two Java Cards

    Use development cards from two vendors and a host library to test signing and recovery. The goal is one signature Bitcoin can verify.

  2. In parallel

    Prototype the companion chip

    Build one signing participant on an FPGA and test it with a compatible card to see what custom hardware can improve.

  3. First paid pilot

    Integrate with a wallet maker

    Choose the pilot hardware around the buyer’s signing, recovery, and security requirements. Build a companion ASIC if customer designs justify it.
